# Famous Decision #1: Steve Jobs — Return to Apple (1997)

**The Decision:** Steve Jobs was ousted from Apple in 1985. In 1997, Apple was 90 days from bankruptcy. They offered him the CEO role back. Should he take it?

*Developed by Hussain Ali — github.com/itshussainsprojects/Claude-Council-Skill*

---

═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
                         THE COUNCIL
        "Should Steve Jobs return to Apple as CEO in 1997?"
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

⚔ THE ADVERSARY
Apple is 90 days from bankruptcy. The board that brought you back is the same institutional culture that fired you twelve years ago — they haven't changed, the situation has just gotten desperate enough that they need a miracle. You're not being brought back because they believe in you. You're being brought back because everyone else failed and there's nobody left. The company has 13 product lines, zero focus, and a stock at $3. You've spent 12 years building NeXT and Pixar — two companies that actually work. Walking into Apple right now means inheriting someone else's catastrophic decisions and betting your reputation that you can fix what a decade of mediocrity broke. The Strategist will tell you about the upside. I'm telling you about the 90% of scenarios where Apple goes bankrupt anyway and you go down with it.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

📈 THE STRATEGIST
The Adversary is describing the floor. I want to describe the ceiling — which is unlike anything in business history. Apple has something almost no bankrupt company has: a fanatically loyal customer base that still believes in the brand even as the company collapses around it. The Mac faithful exist. That's an asset no balance sheet captures. The strategic play here isn't to save Apple as it exists — it's to burn the product line to the ground, keep the brand and the distribution, and rebuild around one or two products that are actually great. Microsoft's $150M investment buys time. The question isn't whether the opportunity is real. It's whether you are the person who can execute the destruction and rebuilding simultaneously.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

🔬 THE SCIENTIST
The base rate for corporate turnarounds at this level of distress is brutal — fewer than 15% of companies 90 days from bankruptcy recover to become industry leaders. The reference class almost uniformly shows that even successful turnarounds take 5-7 years and destroy enormous value before creating it. What's anomalous here — and what would update my probability estimate significantly — is the combination of: an intact brand with genuine consumer loyalty, a specific person with demonstrated product taste returning to their original context, and a technology cycle (the internet) that will create entirely new product categories. Those three factors together have no clean historical parallel. This might genuinely be the exception. The Scientist in me says: the odds are bad. The evidence in this specific case says: the odds might not apply.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

🎨 THE VISIONARY
Everyone is asking "can Apple be saved?" I want to ask a different question: what is Apple actually for? The answer Jobs already knows — and it's not computers. It's the intersection of technology and liberal arts. The company that builds that doesn't exist yet. The internet is about to change everything about how humans interact with devices. Apple under Jobs isn't a turnaround story. It's a founding story with existing infrastructure. The iMac, the iPod, the iPhone — none of these exist yet, but they're inevitable given the right person with the right obsession. Don't go back to save Apple. Go back to build something that has never existed, using Apple's skeleton as the scaffold.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

⚙ THE ENGINEER
The operational reality: Apple has 13 product lines, most of which share no components, no manufacturing efficiencies, and no coherent platform strategy. The engineering org is bloated, demoralized, and has spent a decade shipping products nobody wanted. The first engineering decision — cutting from 13 product lines to 4 — will be the most important and most painful. It will require firing people, canceling projects teams have spent years on, and absorbing the PR damage of appearing to retreat. The Adversary is right that this is inherited catastrophe. The Engineer in me says: the chaos is actually an advantage. When everything is broken, you have permission to rebuild from scratch. The constraint is talent retention — you need to identify the 200 engineers worth keeping before the restructuring panic causes them to leave.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

🧘 THE PHILOSOPHER
There is a question underneath this question that nobody has asked: what did being fired from Apple do to you, and have you resolved it? Because if you return carrying resentment — even justified resentment — it will poison every decision. The board that fired you was wrong. The products that replaced your vision were mediocre. That doesn't mean returning is about vindication. The most dangerous version of this decision is the one where you go back to prove something rather than to build something. The Visionary described what Apple could become. I want to ask: are you capable, right now, of building that — or are you still the person who was fired? Those are different people. Which one shows up in 1997 determines everything.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

❤ THE HUMANIST
Twelve years ago this company broke something in you. I want to name that before we talk strategy. The people who will follow you back into Apple — the engineers, the designers, the believers — they're not following a CEO. They're following a person they think sees something others don't. That relationship is extraordinarily fragile, especially in a crisis. The moment they sense you're there for yourself rather than for the work, you lose them. And you cannot turn Apple around without them. The Philosopher asked if you've resolved the wound. I want to ask if you're capable of the specific kind of leadership this moment requires — which isn't genius, it's presence. Showing up every day for people who are terrified, in a company that feels like it's falling apart, and making them believe the work matters. That's the job. The products are secondary.

═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
                         THE VERDICT
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

POSITION: Take it. But don't go back to save Apple — go back to
          rebuild it as something that has never existed before.
          The turnaround frame is wrong. The founding frame is right.

CONFIDENCE: 79% — The brand loyalty and technology timing are
            genuinely anomalous; drops to 45% if the psychological
            wounds from 1985 haven't been processed and resolved.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

CRITICAL RISKS  (exactly 3)

  1. **Vindication Trap**: Returning to prove the board wrong in 1985
     poisons product decisions — every choice becomes about the past,
     not the future.

  2. **Talent Exodus During Restructuring**: Cutting 13 product lines
     to 4 triggers a panic exodus of the exact engineers needed to
     rebuild — the communication of the vision must precede the cuts.

  3. **Microsoft Dependency**: The $150M from Gates creates a
     perception of defeat that undermines the brand narrative —
     must be reframed publicly as a partnership, not a rescue.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

NEXT STEPS  (exactly 5, in order of priority)

  1. Spend 30 days listening only — meet every team lead, say nothing
     about direction, understand exactly what's broken before cutting.
  2. Identify the 4 product lines that survive — everything else is
     cancelled, no exceptions, no sentiment.
  3. Find the 200 engineers worth keeping and tell them personally
     before any restructuring announcement goes public.
  4. Reframe the Microsoft deal publicly: "We just proved Apple can
     attract strategic partners. We're not dying — we're choosing allies."
  5. Ship one product that is unmistakably, obviously, beautifully Apple
     within 12 months. The iMac. Make it the proof of concept.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

MINORITY REPORT: ⚔ THE ADVERSARY
"The verdict may be historically correct — we know how this ends.
But in 1997, with the information available, the rational decision
was to let Apple die and protect what you'd built at NeXT and Pixar.
Returning was a bet that paid off. It was still a bet."
