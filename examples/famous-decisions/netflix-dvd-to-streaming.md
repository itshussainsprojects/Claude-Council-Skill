# Famous Decision #4: Reed Hastings — Kill the DVD Business for Streaming (2007)

**The Decision:** Netflix had 7 million DVD subscribers and was profitable. Reed Hastings wanted to pivot entirely to streaming — a format with almost no content and slow internet. Should he do it?

*Developed by Hussain Ali — github.com/itshussainsprojects/Claude-Council-Skill*

---

═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
                         THE COUNCIL
    "Should Netflix abandon its profitable DVD business for streaming?"
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

⚔ THE ADVERSARY
You have 7 million paying subscribers, positive cash flow, and a business model that actually works. You want to trade that for a streaming platform with no content, no rights deals, no proven consumer behavior, and an internet infrastructure that buffers constantly for most American households. Blockbuster has 9,000 stores and finally figured out online rentals. Amazon is building a competing DVD service. The threat to your core business is real and present. The streaming opportunity is theoretical and distant. "The future is streaming" is not a strategy — it's an observation. The strategy question is whether you can survive long enough and with enough resources to build that future while protecting the present. Cannibalizing your only profitable business to chase a format that doesn't work yet for most customers is not a pivot. It's a gamble.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

📈 THE STRATEGIST
The Adversary is describing the risk correctly and ignoring the competitive timing entirely. Here is the strategic reality: broadband penetration in the US is crossing 50% and accelerating. In 5 years, the infrastructure problem solves itself. In 10 years, physical media is a nostalgia product. The company that owns streaming habits in 2007 owns the entertainment relationship for the next generation of consumers. If you wait until streaming is obviously viable — until the content is there, until the internet is fast enough for everyone — you wait until the window is closing. Disney, Time Warner, and the studios will build their own streaming services the moment the market is proven. Your only advantage is moving before they realize they need to. The DVD business is not your future. It's your funding mechanism for building the future.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

🔬 THE SCIENTIST
The broadband data the Strategist is citing is correct. Here is additional data worth considering: consumer behavior change of this magnitude — from physical media to digital — has historically followed an S-curve that takes 7-10 years to reach majority adoption once the infrastructure exists. The early movers in format transitions have a mixed record: they capture early adopters but often run out of capital before the mainstream follows. The specific evidence that would update my probability estimate significantly: whether the content rights deals necessary to make streaming viable are achievable at prices that work given your current balance sheet. That's the unknown that determines everything. DVD content rights you have. Streaming rights are a completely separate negotiation, and the studios know exactly what leverage they have.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

🎨 THE VISIONARY
The frame everyone is using is wrong. This isn't "DVD vs streaming." It's "distribution vs platform." DVDs make Netflix a distribution company — a very good one, but structurally limited by physical inventory, shipping, and the studios' control of content windows. Streaming makes Netflix a platform. Platforms have fundamentally different economics, leverage, and ceilings than distribution businesses. The Adversary is comparing the revenue of today's DVD business to the speculative revenue of tomorrow's streaming platform. The Visionary sees that you're not comparing revenue — you're comparing what kind of company you want to be in 2020. Distribution businesses get squeezed. Platforms get to set terms. There is no version of the DVD business where Netflix is the most powerful company in entertainment. There is a version of streaming where you are.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

⚙ THE ENGINEER
The technical constraints the Adversary is citing are real and have specific timelines. Average US broadband in 2007 is 2-3 Mbps — sufficient for SD streaming but not HD, and only for users with stable connections. The CDN infrastructure required to serve streaming at scale doesn't exist in the form you'd need it; you'd essentially be building it. The encoding standards aren't settled. The device ecosystem — smart TVs, streaming boxes, game consoles — is nascent. None of these are permanent problems: they're 3-5 year engineering challenges. The engineering path is clear even if difficult. The bigger technical risk is the content delivery agreements with studios, which are business problems disguised as technical ones. Get the content rights architecture right first — the delivery infrastructure can be built.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

🧘 THE PHILOSOPHER
There's a deeper question here than format or timing. What is Netflix actually for? The DVD business answers: "convenient access to movies." The streaming vision answers: "the world's entertainment, available anywhere, instantly." Those are different missions, and the distance between them is not just technical — it's about what problem you believe you exist to solve. The companies that navigate format transitions successfully almost always do so because they're clear on the mission and willing to let the format serve the mission, rather than protecting the format as if it were the mission. Blockbuster failed not because streaming beat DVDs but because Blockbuster thought it was in the DVD business. The question is whether Netflix knows it's in the entertainment access business — and whether you're willing to let that mission require destroying your current business model.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

❤ THE HUMANIST
The 7 million DVD subscribers are people who built a habit around your service. The transition to streaming will break that habit for many of them — not because they don't want streaming, but because change is friction, and friction causes cancellations. The human cost of getting the transition wrong is measured in subscriber churn, in customer support calls from people whose streaming doesn't work, in the betrayal felt by early loyalists who feel the service they loved became something they didn't ask for. The way you manage this transition — how much you explain, how much you give customers control, how honestly you communicate — will determine whether your existing base follows you or leaves. The Adversary is right that the DVD business works. The Humanist says: treat the customers in it as people whose trust you're asking to transfer, not a revenue line you're managing down.

═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
                         THE VERDICT
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

POSITION: Build streaming aggressively, but do NOT kill the DVD
          business yet. Run both in parallel until streaming reaches
          2 million subscribers — then accelerate the transition.
          The DVD business is your runway, not your enemy.

CONFIDENCE: 76% — The platform economics of streaming are
            compelling and the timing is right; drops to 55%
            if content rights deals with studios can't be
            closed at sustainable margins.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

CRITICAL RISKS  (exactly 3)

  1. **Studio Content Rights Leverage**: The studios understand that
     streaming rights are a new negotiation entirely — they will price
     them to capture most of the value, making the economics unworkable
     unless Netflix eventually produces original content.

  2. **Subscriber Whiplash**: Forcing the transition too fast (the
     Qwikster mistake, before it happens) destroys the trust of 7 million
     customers who chose Netflix specifically for DVDs.

  3. **Competitive Window Closing**: Amazon, Apple, and the studios
     are all watching — the window where Netflix can establish streaming
     habits before well-capitalized competitors enter is 3-4 years, not 10.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

NEXT STEPS  (exactly 5, in order of priority)

  1. Close streaming rights deals with at least 3 major studios in
     the next 6 months — accept bad terms to get content; improve
     terms as leverage grows.
  2. Launch streaming as an included feature of the DVD subscription,
     not a replacement — reduce transition friction to near zero.
  3. Begin original content development planning now — the studio
     leverage problem only resolves when you're also a studio.
  4. Invest in the device ecosystem: get Netflix on game consoles,
     set-top boxes, and TVs before competitors do.
  5. Set a clear internal milestone: when streaming hours exceed DVD
     hours per subscriber, begin the transition communication.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

MINORITY REPORT: ⚔ THE ADVERSARY
"The verdict is strategically sound. The historical record shows
the Qwikster experiment — the premature DVD separation — nearly
destroyed the company. The Council's instinct to use DVD as
runway rather than enemy would have avoided the worst mistake
of the actual execution."
